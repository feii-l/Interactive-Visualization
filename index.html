Locked In or Still Avoidable?

How much of each city's warming is already locked in, how much still depends on the emissions we choose, and how much do the climate models disagree?

30 cities, 4 CMIP6 models, three scenarios: the historical run from 1950 to 2014, low emissions (SSP1-2.6) and high emissions (SSP5-8.5) to 2100. The variable is monthly near surface air temperature (tas, member r1i1p1f1). For each city we take the model grid cell nearest to its coordinates, average the twelve months into one value per year, and convert Kelvin to Celsius. Every value is then shown as the change from that city's own 1981 to 2010 average under that same model. That step removes the difference between hot cities and cold cities, and it removes each model's own bias, which is what makes the models comparable. Lines are the median across models and shaded bands span the lowest to the highest model. The three numbers and the ranking use the 2071 to 2100 average.

Write-up

Why this question

Our first charts all said the same obvious thing, that the world gets warmer. The two charts that surprised us were the gap between the low and high emissions scenarios, and how far apart the models were for the same city. Those two numbers are what a reader can actually act on or worry about, so we built the page around them. The framing we settled on splits each city's future into a part that is already locked in, which happens even if emissions fall fast, and a part that is still avoidable.

Design rationale

We show warming as a change from each city's own 1981 to 2010 average instead of raw temperature. Raw temperature puts Singapore near 27 degrees and Reykjavik near 5, so the y axis would be about which city is hot, not about how much it changes. The anomaly also cancels each model's own bias, which is what makes the models comparable at all.

Position on a common scale carries the main quantity in both the line chart and the ranked bars, because position is the channel people read most accurately. Colour carries the scenario, blue for low emissions and orange for high, and the same two colours are reused for the map ramp so the reader never has to relearn them. The map uses colour for magnitude only as a secondary view, since 30 points on a world map is good for spotting the pattern by region but bad for comparing two cities, which is what the bar chart is for.

Model spread is a shaded band rather than a separate line for each of the four models. Drawing every model at once was unreadable in our exploratory version, so the band is the default and the individual models are behind a toggle. That keeps the overplotting available when a reader wants it without making it the first thing they see.

We considered a small multiple of all 30 cities and a heatmap of city by year. The small multiples were too small to read the band, and the heatmap hid the scenario gap, which is the whole point of the page. We also tried animating the map on its own and found that people could not tell how much of the change was the scenario and how much was the year, so the year slider and the scenario toggle are now separate controls.

Interactions

The page opens with three findings. Clicking one sets the map, the line chart and the ranking to the view that shows it, scrolls the reader there, and explains what to look at. Touching any control clears that note, so the guided path and free exploration use the same page instead of two separate modes.

Clicking a city on the map or a bar in the ranking selects it, and all three views stay in sync. The year slider repaints the map and moves a marker in the line chart, and the play button runs the years from 1950 to 2100, so a reader can watch where warming shows up first. The scenario toggle switches which future the map shows. The measure dropdown re-sorts the 30 cities four ways: total warming under high emissions, warming locked in, warming still avoidable, and model disagreement. A checkbox swaps the shaded band for one line per model, which is how a reader can see the overplotting we hid by default. Hovering gives details on demand in both charts, and a table at the bottom lists every number for readers who want them.

Development process

Fei wrote the data notebook. It pulls yearly temperature for the 30 cities from the CMIP6 store on Google Cloud, computes each city's change from its 1981 to 2010 average, and makes our six exploratory charts. Alina built the D3 page: the map, the line chart with the model band, the ranked bars, and the three guided findings. Abigail tested the page across browsers and wrote the write-up.

We spent about 5 hours in total. The part that took the longest was the interactive D3 page, especially keeping the map, line chart and bar chart in sync when a city is selected, and getting the year animation and tooltips to work smoothly. Getting the data was the second slowest part. Each model and scenario has to be downloaded separately, and two of the six models we planned to use failed: MPI-ESM1-2-LR had a date-indexing error, and NorESM2-LM had no low-emissions run. We kept the four models that had all three scenarios.
