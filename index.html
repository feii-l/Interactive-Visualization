Locked In or Still Avoidable?

How much of each city's warming is already locked in, how much still depends on the emissions we choose, and how much do the climate models disagree?

30 cities, 4 CMIP6 models, three scenarios: the historical run from 1950 to 2014, low emissions (SSP1-2.6) and high emissions (SSP5-8.5) to 2100. The variable is monthly near surface air temperature (tas, member r1i1p1f1). For each city we take the model grid cell nearest to its coordinates, average the twelve months into one value per year, and convert Kelvin to Celsius. Every value is then shown as the change from that city's own 1981 to 2010 average under that same model. That step removes the difference between hot cities and cold cities, and it removes each model's own bias, which is what makes the models comparable. Lines are the median across models and shaded bands span the lowest to the highest model. The three numbers and the ranking use the 2071 to 2100 average.

Write-up

Why this question

When we made our first exploratory charts, almost all of them told us that the world is getting warmer. That wasn't very surprising. Two charts did surprise us, though. The first showed how big the gap is between the low and high emissions scenarios, and the second showed how much the climate models disagree about the very same city. Those felt like the numbers a reader could actually worry about, so we built the whole page around them. In the end, we split each city's future into two parts. One part is already locked in and will happen even if emissions drop quickly. The other part is still avoidable, depending on the choices we make.

Design rationale

Instead of raw temperature, we show warming as the change from each city's own 1981 to 2010 average. Raw temperatures would put Singapore near 27 degrees and Reykjavik near 5. The chart would then be about which city is hotter, not how much each one is changing. Measuring change also cancels out each model's built-in bias, which is what lets us compare the models fairly.

For the line chart and the ranked bars, we used position on a shared scale for the main number, since position is the easiest thing for people to read accurately. Color shows the scenario, with blue for low emissions and orange for high emissions. We reused those same two colors on the map so readers wouldn't have to learn a new color scheme halfway through. On the map, color shows how much each city warms. The map is nice for spotting patterns by region, but it's not great for comparing two specific cities, so that's what the bar chart is for.

For model spread, we went with a shaded band instead of drawing a separate line for each of the four models. In our early version we drew every model at once, and it was honestly pretty hard to read. Now the band is the default, and the individual model lines are behind a checkbox. That way they're still there for anyone curious, but they aren't the first thing you see.

We tried a few other designs along the way. Small multiples of all 30 cities made each chart too small to read the band. A heatmap of city by year hid the gap between scenarios, which is really the main point of the page. We also tried animating the map on its own, but people couldn't tell how much of the change came from the scenario and how much came from the year. That's why the year slider and the scenario toggle ended up as separate controls.

Interactions

The page opens with three findings. Clicking one sets the map, the line chart, and the ranking to the view that shows it, scrolls you to the right spot, and explains what to look for. As soon as you touch any control, that note goes away. We liked this setup because the guided tour and free exploration happen on the same page.

You can select a city by clicking it on the map or clicking its bar in the ranking. All three views update together. The year slider recolors the map and moves a marker along the line chart. If you press Play, it runs through every year from 1950 to 2100, so you can watch where warming shows up first. The scenario toggle switches which future the map shows. The dropdown lets you rank the 30 cities by total warming under high emissions, warming that's locked in, warming that's still avoidable, or model disagreement. There's also a checkbox that swaps the shaded band for one line per model. Hovering over any chart gives you the details, and if you want every number, there's a table at the bottom.

Development process

Fei wrote the data notebook, which pulls yearly temperatures for the 30 cities from the CMIP6 store on Google Cloud. The notebook also calculates each city's change from its 1981 to 2010 average and makes our six exploratory charts. Fei also helped with debugging the page. Alina built the D3 page, including the map, the line chart with the model band, the ranked bars, and the three guided findings. Abigail tested the page in different browsers and wrote the write-up.

Altogether we spent about 15 people-hours, or roughly 5 hours each. The interactive D3 page took the longest. Keeping the map, line chart, and bar chart in sync when someone selects a city was the trickiest part. Getting the year animation and tooltips to run smoothly took a while too. We also spent a good chunk of time debugging. For example, some of the map circles were hard to click, especially while the animation was playing, and the marker on the line chart didn't follow the mouse at first. Getting the data was the second slowest part. Each model and scenario had to be downloaded separately, and two of the six models we planned to use didn't work out. MPI-ESM1-2-LR had a date indexing error, and NorESM2-LM didn't have a low emissions run. In the end, we kept the four models that had all three scenarios.
